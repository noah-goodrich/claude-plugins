# Being Told I Could Be Fired Was the Best Thing for My Career

Recently I had the privilege of going to lunch with a long-time manager of mine who has become a very close friend. We talked about a lot of things, and the conversation ended with a simple, yet complicated to answer question to my friend. I asked, "Its evident that you do care, but why?" His answer was deep and insightful and yet again taught me something I will take with me as I leave his employ to pursue a new adventure. I'll have to write about that answer another time.

Shortly after as I was scanning my LInkedIn feed I stumbled on a short post where the author outlined the first two principles of organizational psychology as they understand them:

- Never, ever mention firing people and avoid it if possible
- Recognize its always leadership's fault

You may be asking yourself, "what do those two things have to do with each other?"

## I was told I would be fired

Eight years ago, I sat across from that very same manager as he told me I'd have to learn to be a team player or he'd have to let me go. Walking out of that meeting, I knew three things: I had some big changes to make, it was going to take work, and my boss believed wholeheartedly that I could do it. Admittedly I hadn't even heard the term organizational psychology until I read this post and I only ever took a single class in organizational behavior when I was in college so I'm clearly no expert in the field. But I'm going to hazard a guess here that the first tenet this author mentioned is a little more nuanced than suggesting that absolutely, under no circumstances should a leader ever tell an employee they could be fired.

## Sheldon Cooper Syndrome

I'd like to provide a little backstory here first. Growing up I was one of those kids that naturally succeeded in school and was continually reminded how gifted, talented, and smart I was. During that period any time I came up against a situation where I was working with a team and failure looked likely I simply quit the team. Being a fan of Big Bang Theory I can only best explain this as I had Sheldon Cooper Syndrome, except I wasn't nearly as smart and I was a lot more interested in girls and making friends 🙂. Unfortunately the lessons I failed to learn during adolescence followed me into adulthood and my professional career. Imagine all the things that make Sheldon Cooper hard to work with and you wouldn't be that far off.

## He did what was needed for me and for the team

At the time this conversation with my manager took place I'd been working for him for a while and he'd had plenty of opportunity to observe the way I worked with the rest of the team. His feedback was totally spot on. And by letting me know the consequences for failure, he did the only thing he could for the health of the team. Frankly, I would suggest that had he failed to have this conversation with me, that would have been a failure of leadership.

## There's a WRONG and a RIGHT Way

I'm pretty sure we can all think of those workplaces that have gained notoriety for their use of the vitality curve in their employee evaluations and performance reviews. These companies aren't really any different from watching The Apprentice where a famous businessman-turned-politician created a dog-eat-dog competition.

Such cultures breed fear and paranoia and ultimately work counter to the interests of building highly effective teams.

Conversely let's consider one of the most famous teams in the entire world, the Navy SEALS. Author Simon Sinek has written and spoken extensively about what he's learned from interviewing military leaders. He shares the following diagram of how to build highly effective teams:

My two cents here is that you can only build highly effective teams if you build Trust. You can only build Trust if there is Accountability. And Accountability can only exist when there are real Consequences. In the SEALS, the consequences are always immediately apparent. If a team member fails in their duty, one of their brothers-in-arms may not make it home.

Fortunately in the world of business the stakes aren't as high nor the consequences as dire. However this means that as leaders and team members we have to observe and be ready to provide feedback as necessary. Part of that feedback is helping your team members understand what failure looks like and how to avoid it. In the world of business firing an employee should be a last resort, but if that's what you're considering doing then it can be a kindness to share that truth with the employee facing firing.

## I owe all my successes to that conversation

That wasn't the last crucial conversation that manager and I had to have either. Over the years it became very clear to me that while we weren't the type of friends that hung out or went to get drinks together after work, he became one of the best friends I have ever had. The most important thing that he brought to our friendship was the courage to always be completely honest and perfectly frank, even when it offended, hurt, or scared me.

As a final thought I recently started an awesome new job working with one of those former teammates. And this past August I celebrated my seventeenth anniversary with my best friend. All because a leader had the courage and kindness to tell me he was going to have to fire me.
