## Key Findings

- A systematic review is defined by *pre-specified eligibility criteria* answering a *specific*
  question, using *explicit, systematic methods selected to minimize bias* — this is the
  load-bearing difference from narrative/ad-hoc review.
- The mechanism that makes it work is structural: an a priori protocol fixes the question, scope,
  eligibility, search, and analysis *before* the studies are known, so reviewer bias cannot steer
  the conclusion.
- "Post hoc decisions made when the impact on the results of the research is known [...] are highly
  susceptible to bias and should therefore be avoided" — the same logic as study preregistration,
  applied to evidence synthesis.
- SRs exist to solve a scale problem: the literature is too large for any individual decision-maker
  to assess unaided, so a reproducible synthesis is required to inform decisions.

## Verified Quote(s)

**Location reference:** Section 1.1 ("Why do a systematic review?") and Section 1.5 ("Protocol
development"), Cochrane Handbook current version, Chapter 1.

> A systematic review attempts to collate all the empirical evidence that fits pre-specified
> eligibility criteria in order to answer a specific research question. It uses explicit, systematic
> methods that are selected with a view to minimizing bias, thus providing more reliable findings
> from which conclusions can be drawn and decisions made.

> Publication of a protocol for a review that is written without knowledge of the available studies
> reduces the impact of review authors' biases, promotes transparency of methods and processes,
> reduces the potential for duplication, allows peer review of the planned methods before they have
> been completed.

> Post hoc decisions made when the impact on the results of the research is known, such as excluding
> selected studies from a systematic review, or changing the statistical analysis, are highly
> susceptible to bias and should therefore be avoided.
