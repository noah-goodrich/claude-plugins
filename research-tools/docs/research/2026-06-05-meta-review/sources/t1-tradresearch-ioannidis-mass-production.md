## Key Findings

- The headline contrarian claim: "Possibly, the large majority of produced systematic reviews and
  meta-analyses are unnecessary, misleading, and/or conflicted." Rigor at the method level does not
  guarantee value at the population level — the system is being gamed.
- Scale is documented empirically: PubMed tagged 266,782 systematic reviews and 58,611 meta-analyses
  (1986–2015); annual SR output grew 2,728% (1991–2014) vs only 153% for all PubMed items. "Probably
  more systematic reviews of trials than new randomized trials are published annually."
- Redundancy: same-topic meta-analyses "may exceed 20 sometimes"; 185 meta-analyses of
  antidepressants for depression were published 2007–2014.
- Conflicts of interest: many SRs/MAs are produced by industry employees or industry-tied authors and
  "results are aligned with sponsor interests" — the synthesis layer inherits and can amplify bias.
- Crucially, Ioannidis does NOT reject the method: it is "indispensable" and "irrational not to
  systematically review what is already known." The failure is incentives and overproduction, not the
  methodology itself.

**Location reference:** Abstract — "Policy Points" and "Conclusions"; and the body section "Increase

> Currently, there is massive production of unnecessary, misleading, and conflicted systematic
> reviews and meta-analyses. Instead of promoting evidence-based medicine and health care, these
> instruments often serve mostly as easily produced publishable units or marketing tools.

> Possibly, the large majority of produced systematic reviews and meta-analyses are unnecessary,
> misleading, and/or conflicted.

> In the period January 1, 1986, to December 4, 2015, PubMed tags 266,782 items as "systematic
> reviews" and 58,611 as "meta-analyses." Annual publications between 1991 and 2014 increased 2,728%
> for systematic reviews and 2,635% for meta-analyses versus only 153% for all PubMed-indexed items.

> In other words, it is irrational not to systematically review what is already known before deciding
> to perform any new study.
