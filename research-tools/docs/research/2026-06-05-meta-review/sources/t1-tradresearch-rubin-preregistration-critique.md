## Key Findings

- Central contrarian claim: preregistration's *historical* transparency (did you deviate from your
  plan?) does not, by itself, improve credibility judgments when researchers already provide
  *contemporary* transparency — clear rationales, open data/materials/code, and robustness checks.
- The distinction Rubin draws — historical vs contemporary transparency — reframes the entire
  preregistration debate: what makes findings credible is the *current* justification and
  reproducibility, not a time-stamped record of intentions.
- Preregistration does not fix the deeper problems (poor theory, weak inference, hidden moderators);
  it targets analytical flexibility but not the substantive reasoning that drives replication failure.
- Honest concession: historical transparency IS necessary to judge whether a researcher's current
  approach is better or worse than the planned one — so the critique is "overstated benefits," not
  "no value."
