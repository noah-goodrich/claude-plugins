# Evidence Hierarchy

Classify every source into one of 9 levels. This classification is recorded alongside the
credibility score but is a **separate axis** — a brilliant anecdotal account (Level 8) can
score high on credibility if it's honest, specific, and corroborated. The hierarchy indicates
the TYPE of evidence, not its quality.

---

## The Hierarchy

### Level 1: Systematic Review / Meta-Analysis
**What it is:** A study that combines results from multiple independent studies on the same
question using a defined, reproducible methodology.

**Why it's at the top:** Aggregates evidence across studies, reducing the impact of any single
study's flaws. The closest thing to "settled science" in social science.

**Watch for:** Garbage in, garbage out — if the underlying studies are weak, the meta-analysis
is weak. Check what studies were included.

**Example:** "A meta-analysis of 14 studies on budgeting app retention found that..."

---

### Level 2: Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT)
**What it is:** A study where participants are randomly assigned to groups (e.g., one group uses
a budgeting tool, the control group doesn't) and outcomes are compared.

**Why it ranks high:** Random assignment controls for confounding variables. Demonstrates
causation, not just correlation.

**Watch for:** Small sample sizes; short duration; self-selection bias in recruitment;
artificial conditions that don't match real life.

**Example:** "Participants randomly assigned to use the envelope method saved 12% more than the
control group over 6 months."

---

### Level 3: Large-Scale Observational Study / Longitudinal Research
**What it is:** A study that observes a large population over time without intervening. Includes
surveys, cohort studies, and analysis of existing data.

**Why it ranks here:** Large samples reveal real-world patterns. Longitudinal design shows how
behavior changes over time.

**Watch for:** Correlation vs. causation. Selection bias. Self-reported data may not match
actual behavior.

**Example:** "The Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances found that 37% of US households
could not cover a $400 emergency expense."

---

### Level 4: Expert Consensus / Professional Body Guidance
**What it is:** Official positions, guidelines, or standards published by professional
organizations or regulatory bodies.

**Why it ranks here:** Represents the collective judgment of qualified experts. Usually
developed through structured review processes.

**Watch for:** Institutional bias (protecting the profession); slow to update; may reflect
the median expert view rather than the best evidence.

**Example:** "The AFCPE recommends that financial counselors assess client money scripts
before prescribing budgeting methods."

---

### Level 5: Practitioner Case Study with Data
**What it is:** A detailed analysis of a specific intervention, program, or approach, supported
by measurable outcomes.

**Why it ranks here:** Real-world application with actual data. More grounded than theory.

**Watch for:** Cherry-picked success stories; survivorship bias; sample of one (or very few).

**Example:** "Over 3 years, our financial coaching practice saw 240 clients transition from
paycheck-to-paycheck to having 3+ months' expenses saved. Here's the data..."

---

### Level 6: Qualitative Research / Interviews
**What it is:** In-depth exploration of experiences, attitudes, and meanings through interviews,
focus groups, or ethnographic observation.

**Why it ranks here:** Reveals the WHY behind behavior that quantitative data misses. Rich
context.

**Watch for:** Small samples; researcher interpretation bias; findings may not generalize.

**Example:** "In interviews with 20 couples, we found that the person who 'owned' the budget
spreadsheet consistently reported lower financial anxiety."

---

### Level 7: Expert Opinion / Thought Leadership
**What it is:** Analysis, frameworks, or recommendations from recognized experts, not backed by
formal research in this specific piece.

**Why it ranks here:** Informed by deep experience and domain knowledge, but this particular
piece is opinion rather than evidence.

**Watch for:** Halo effect (famous expert ≠ right on every topic); opinions presented as facts;
extrapolation beyond expertise.

**Example:** "Morgan Housel argues in 'The Psychology of Money' that the most important
financial skill is getting the goalpost to stop moving."

---

### Level 8: Anecdotal / Personal Experience
**What it is:** First-person accounts of what worked or didn't work for a specific individual
or household.

**Why it ranks here:** Valuable for hypothesis generation and illustrating lived experience.
Not generalizable on its own.

**Watch for:** Survivorship bias (you hear from people it worked for); unique circumstances;
confounding factors the author isn't aware of.

**Example:** "We tried envelope budgeting for a year. Here's exactly what happened, including
the month we blew through every envelope by the 20th."

---

### Level 9: Marketing / Promotional Content
**What it is:** Content whose primary purpose is to sell a product, service, or ideology.

**Why it's at the bottom:** The content exists to persuade, not to inform. Claims are selected
for marketing impact, not accuracy.

**When to keep it anyway:** Sometimes marketing content reveals genuine user needs, product
design philosophy, or market positioning that is analytically useful — not because the claims
are trustworthy, but because the FRAMING reveals something about what users want.

**Example:** "Our budgeting app helped users save an average of $600/month!" (no methodology,
no sample details, clear sales intent)

---

## Using the Hierarchy

1. **Classify every source** before scoring it on the 10 credibility dimensions
2. **Note the classification** on the source evaluation card
3. **Use it during synthesis** — when sources at different levels disagree, explain the
   evidence differential
4. **Don't use it as a gate** — a Level 8 source with a credibility score of 7.5 contributes
   more than a Level 3 source with a credibility score of 4.0
5. **The hierarchy informs the Evidence Quality dimension** (Dimension 2 in the rubric) but
   does not replace it — a poorly executed RCT (Level 2) can still score low on Evidence
   Quality
