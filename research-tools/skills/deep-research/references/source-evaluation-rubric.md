# Source Evaluation Rubric

10 dimensions, each scored 0–10. Use the anchors below to justify your score — do not score
intuitively. Find the anchor that best matches the source, then adjust ±1 based on specifics.

**Bias guard:** If you agree strongly with a source's conclusions, score it harder on dimensions
5, 6, and 8. If you disagree, score it more generously on those same dimensions. This
counteracts confirmation bias in both directions.

---

## 1. Authority (25% weight)

Who created this, and are they qualified to speak on this topic?

| Score | Anchor | Examples |
|-------|--------|----------|
| 9–10 | Recognized expert with deep domain credentials | Tenured professor publishing in their field; licensed CFP with 20+ years practice; peer-reviewed journal article |
| 7–8 | Credentialed professional or reputable institution | Government agency (CFPB, Fed); professional body (AFCPE, FPA); established financial publication (WSJ, FT) |
| 5–6 | Experienced practitioner or established voice | Well-known blogger with track record (Mr. Money Mustache); fintech founder with demonstrated results; certified coach |
| 3–4 | General media or non-specialist author | News outlet covering finance as one beat; general-interest magazine; popular book by non-specialist |
| 1–2 | Anonymous, unverifiable, or no relevant credentials | Anonymous forum post; social media thread; content mill article with no byline |

**Key question:** Could you verify this person's qualifications independently?

---

## 2. Evidence Quality (20% weight)

How rigorous is the evidence behind the claims?

| Score | Anchor | Examples |
|-------|--------|----------|
| 9–10 | Systematic review, meta-analysis, or well-designed RCT | Cochrane review; multi-year longitudinal study with control group; large-scale randomized experiment |
| 7–8 | Large-scale observational study or rigorous survey | Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances; Pew Research study; NBER working paper with solid methodology |
| 5–6 | Expert consensus, professional body guidance, or mixed-methods study | CFPB guidelines; AFCPE position papers; industry survey with transparent methodology |
| 3–4 | Case studies, qualitative research, or small samples | Interview-based study (n<30); single-organization case study; pilot program results |
| 1–2 | Anecdotal, opinion-only, or no evidence cited | "In my experience..."; unsourced claims; testimonials; "everyone knows that..." |

**Key question:** If someone challenged this claim, what evidence could the author point to?

---

## 3. Currency (10% weight)

How recent is the publication AND the underlying data?

| Score | Anchor | Examples |
|-------|--------|----------|
| 9–10 | Published within 1 year; data within 2 years | 2025-2026 publication using 2024+ data |
| 7–8 | Published within 3 years; data within 5 years | 2023-2024 publication; survey data from 2021+ |
| 5–6 | Published within 5 years; data within 7 years | 2021-2022 publication; still-relevant analysis |
| 3–4 | Published 5-10 years ago; data may be outdated | Pre-2021 work; economic conditions have shifted |
| 1–2 | Published 10+ years ago; context has changed substantially | Pre-2016 work; pre-smartphone-era assumptions |

**Timeless bonus (+2, cap at 10):** Principles that don't depend on economic conditions or
technology (e.g., "spend less than you earn," cognitive bias research, relationship dynamics)
get a +2 bonus. The underlying data may be old, but the insight is evergreen.

**Key question:** Has the world changed enough since this was written to invalidate it?

---

## 4. Intent (10% weight)

Why was this content created? What does the creator get out of it?

| Score | Anchor | Examples |
|-------|--------|----------|
| 9–10 | Pure education, academic inquiry, or public service | University research; government consumer protection content; non-profit financial education |
| 7–8 | Professional development or field advancement | Conference presentations; professional journal articles; open-source tool documentation |
| 5–6 | Thought leadership with indirect commercial interest | Blog by a financial advisor (builds reputation, may attract clients); fintech company's research arm |
| 3–4 | Content marketing with some genuine value | NerdWallet comparison articles; bank-sponsored "financial wellness" content; app company blog |
| 1–2 | Direct product sales, lead generation, or propaganda | "Sign up now" landing pages; affiliate-heavy reviews; ideologically motivated content disguised as research |

**Key question:** If this content generated zero revenue or attention, would the creator still
have published it?

---

## 5. Bias & Objectivity (10% weight)

How balanced is the presentation? Does it acknowledge the other side?

| Score | Anchor | Examples |
|-------|--------|----------|
| 9–10 | Presents multiple perspectives; discloses limitations and conflicts | Academic paper with limitations section; review article comparing approaches fairly |
| 7–8 | Generally balanced; minor blind spots but not misleading | Well-researched journalism; professional guidance that acknowledges trade-offs |
| 5–6 | Has a clear perspective but doesn't misrepresent the opposition | Advocate for a specific methodology who still cites counterevidence |
| 3–4 | Noticeably one-sided; omits inconvenient evidence | Guru pushing their system without mentioning failure rates; cherry-picked data |
| 1–2 | Overtly biased; strawmans opposition; suppresses contradictory evidence | Infomercial-style content; ideological screed; deliberate misinformation |

**Key question:** Does this source ever say "on the other hand" or "this doesn't work for
everyone"?

---

## 6. Logic & Coherence (5% weight)

Is the reasoning sound? Do the conclusions follow from the evidence?

| Score | Anchor | Examples |
|-------|--------|----------|
| 9–10 | Airtight reasoning; conclusions clearly supported by presented evidence | Well-structured argument with explicit logical steps; statistical analysis with appropriate caveats |
| 7–8 | Generally sound; minor leaps but nothing misleading | Most claims supported; occasional generalization from limited data acknowledged |
| 5–6 | Mostly logical but some unsupported jumps | Mixes well-supported claims with opinions stated as facts; correlation/causation conflation |
| 3–4 | Significant logical gaps; emotional reasoning substituted for evidence | Appeals to authority or emotion; "it worked for me therefore it works"; survivorship bias |
| 1–2 | Internally contradictory or fundamentally flawed reasoning | Claims contradict each other; circular reasoning; unfalsifiable assertions |

**Key question:** If you removed the author's charisma and just read the argument on paper,
would it hold up?

---

## 7. Corroboration (5% weight)

Do other credible, independent sources confirm the key claims?

| Score | Anchor | Examples |
|-------|--------|----------|
| 9–10 | Widely confirmed across multiple independent credible sources | Claim appears in textbooks, government guidance, AND practitioner experience |
| 7–8 | Confirmed by 2-3 independent credible sources | Academic study + professional body + established journalist all reach same conclusion |
| 5–6 | Partially confirmed; some claims verified, others unchecked | Key insight confirmed by one other source; peripheral claims unverified |
| 3–4 | Limited confirmation; mostly stands alone | Only one other source makes this claim, and that source may not be independent |
| 1–2 | Uncorroborated; no independent confirmation found | Unique claim with no supporting evidence from other sources |

**Key question:** If this source disappeared, would the same conclusion still be reachable
from other sources?

---

## 8. Intellectual Honesty (5% weight)

Does the source acknowledge what it doesn't know?

| Score | Anchor | Examples |
|-------|--------|----------|
| 9–10 | Explicitly acknowledges uncertainty, limitations, and counterarguments | "Our study has these limitations..."; "This approach may not work for households that..."; "We don't yet know..." |
| 7–8 | Generally honest; doesn't overclaim | Qualifies generalizations; notes when sample size is small; mentions exceptions |
| 5–6 | Some acknowledgment but tends toward confidence | Mentions limitations briefly but doesn't dwell; frames findings positively |
| 3–4 | Overclaims; presents uncertain findings as definitive | "The answer is simple..."; "Always do X..."; "This is the only way..." |
| 1–2 | Actively misleading about certainty; suppresses doubt | Guarantees outcomes; dismisses all criticism; presents ideology as settled science |

**Key question:** Does this source ever say "I don't know" or "it depends"?

---

## 9. Specificity (5% weight)

Does the source provide concrete data, examples, and actionable detail?

| Score | Anchor | Examples |
|-------|--------|----------|
| 9–10 | Rich with specific data, named examples, reproducible methodology | "In our study of 2,400 households, 67% who used envelope budgeting maintained it for 12+ months" |
| 7–8 | Good specificity; most claims backed by concrete detail | Provides numbers, timelines, named tools; methodology is described if not fully reproducible |
| 5–6 | Mix of specific and general; some claims well-supported, others vague | Key points have data; supporting points are hand-waved |
| 3–4 | Mostly general; few concrete details | "Many people find that..."; "Studies show..."; "Experts agree..." without naming them |
| 1–2 | Entirely vague; no concrete data or examples | Platitudes; motivational language; no numbers, no names, no methods |

**Key question:** Could you fact-check the specific claims in this source?

---

## 10. Relevance (5% weight)

How directly does this source address the research question at hand?

| Score | Anchor | Examples |
|-------|--------|----------|
| 9–10 | Directly addresses the exact research question with applicable findings | Study on household budgeting tools when researching household budgeting tools |
| 7–8 | Closely related; requires minor translation to apply | Study on individual budgeting applied to household context; adjacent domain with clear parallels |
| 5–6 | Tangentially related; useful for context but not core findings | General behavioral economics applied to money; psychology of habits applied to financial habits |
| 3–4 | Loosely connected; requires significant interpretation | Corporate budgeting practices applied to personal finance; unrelated domain analogy |
| 1–2 | Barely relevant; included only for completeness | Passing mention in a broader article; different country/culture with limited transferability |

**Key question:** If you had to explain to a skeptic why this source matters to YOUR research
question, could you do it in one sentence?

---

## Computing the Score Band

Compute the weighted average using these weights — but report it as a **3-bucket band**,
not a 2-decimal composite. A score like `7.95` implies a precision a single LLM scoring
prose cannot deliver, and in practice the 2-decimal composite never rejects anything: it
gets back-filled to justify an inclusion decision already made on vibes. The band forces an
honest keep / borderline / reject judgment.

| Dimension | Weight |
|-----------|--------|
| Authority | 25% |
| Evidence Quality | 20% |
| Intent | 10% |
| Currency | 10% |
| Bias & Objectivity | 10% |
| Logic & Coherence | 5% |
| Corroboration | 5% |
| Intellectual Honesty | 5% |
| Specificity | 5% |
| Relevance | 5% |

**Weighted average (intermediate, NOT reported to 2 decimals):**
`(Authority × 0.25) + (Evidence × 0.20) + (Intent × 0.10) + (Currency × 0.10)
+ (Bias × 0.10) + (Logic × 0.05) + (Corroboration × 0.05) + (Honesty × 0.05) +
(Specificity × 0.05) + (Relevance × 0.05)`

The weights reflect a hierarchy: WHO said it and HOW WELL they proved it matter most. WHY they
said it and WHEN they said it matter next. Everything else is refinement.

**Map the weighted average to ONE of three bands:**

| Band | Weighted average | Disposition |
|------|------------------|-------------|
| **keep** | ≥ 7.0 | Strong enough to include without reservation. |
| **borderline** | 5.0 – 6.9 | Include only with a documented reason (gap-fill / sole source) OR cut. Name the call. |
| **reject** | < 5.0 | Cut. Do not back-fill a higher score to rescue it. |

Report the band word (`keep` / `borderline` / `reject`) on the source card — not a
2-decimal number. The intermediate weighted average may be noted in the justification, but
the disposition is the band.

**The real-cut rule.** Every research run must EITHER exclude at least one source OR
explicitly name the lowest-scoring source that cleared the bar (the weakest `keep`, or the
`borderline` source you chose to include and why). A run that includes every source it
evaluated, with no cut and no named marginal keep, is making the inclusion decision on
vibes — the executable gate (Assertion 12) hard-fails it. If the corpus genuinely contains
no source worth cutting, say so by naming the lowest one that survived and defending the
keep.
